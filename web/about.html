About this atlas

What would it cost to build and run an AI data center in each of the 3,144 US counties, and what does that mean for the carbon and water behind every AI answer? This site lets you explore those numbers and change the main assumptions yourself. It presents estimates; it does not recommend where data centers should or should not be built.

Disclaimer. Descriptive information, not a siting recommendation; associations are not causal effects.

Research preview (v0.5.1), accompanying a working paper on where AI is not born equally: the cost of AI data centers across U.S. counties All parameters are provisional and under review. Numbers may change.

What the map shows

MetricIn plain words
10-year cost ($)Cost of building a data center with 100 MW of computing load (adjustable) and running it for ten years: construction, land, grid connection, natural-hazard allowance, electricity, a carbon price, water, staff and a surcharge where water is scarce. Future costs are discounted at 5% per year. It does not include the computers themselves (servers, GPUs), which cost about the same everywhere.
Cost IndexThe 10-year cost divided by the national median, times 50. A county at 60 is 20% more expensive than the typical county; at 40, 20% cheaper. It is not capped at 100.
Price per 1M tokensA cost-based price: the typical market price of a million word-pieces ("tokens") of AI output, plus the difference between this county's facility cost and the national median. It is not what AI providers charge (their prices are set nationally). It mixes open-weight and closed models (slider) and is shown with its difference from the national median. Location moves this price by only about 3%; the choice of model moves it more than five-fold.
Percentile and uncertaintyPercentile 0 = cheapest county, 100 = most expensive. The uncertainty range is the 5th–95th percentile of the county's position across 2,000 simulations in which seven uncertain assumptions vary together (carbon price, water surcharge, construction cost, discount rate, staff, climate effect on cooling, land price); it refers to the baseline scenario (air cooling, 100 MW).
CO₂ per 1M tokensEmissions from the electricity used, at the average emission rate of the regional grid (EPA eGRID 2023).
Scarcity-weighted waterWater evaporated for cooling on site plus water used by the power plants that supply the electricity, weighted by how stressed local water resources are (WRI Aqueduct). 1 L-eq is one litre in a county of median water stress; the same litre counts more where water is scarce.

Themes

  • Cost: 10-year cost, Cost Index and the main cost driver of each county.
  • Token footprint: cost-based price, CO₂ and scarcity-weighted water per 1M tokens.
  • Cheap vs clean: 3 × 3 maps of facility cost against CO₂ or water per token (national thirds).
  • Who bears it: one campus as a share of the regional grid's electricity sales; how rankings change when the social cost of carbon and a water shadow price are counted.
  • Context: social vulnerability, household income and the 2024 presidential vote (two-party share). These are context, not causal: they are not used in the cost model and do not explain its results. The vote layer is shown for information only, in neutral (non-party) colours.
  • Confidence: how much a county's position moves across 2,000 simulations of the uncertain assumptions.

Change vs baseline maps how the current slider settings move each county's cost (%) or percentile compared with the baseline scenario. The optional 3D view raises each county by the mapped value; use the flat map for precise comparison, because perspective distorts heights and hides counties.

What the sliders change

  • Open-weight share: share of tokens from open-weight models (cheaper, more energy-efficient on average) vs closed frontier models. Baseline 30%.
  • Carbon price: $ per tonne of CO₂ added to the electricity bill. Baseline $50; $190 is the US EPA (2023) estimate of the social cost of carbon.
  • Cooling: air/evaporative (baseline) or liquid cooling with dry coolers, which uses far less water and slightly less energy but costs more to build.
  • Water-stress surcharge: extra operating cost in water-stressed counties (0–50%; baseline 30% at the highest stress).
  • Campus size: 10, 100 or 500 MW of computing load.

Everything is recomputed in your browser for all counties. The calculations are a direct translation of the research code (Python), checked to agree within 0.1% for every county.

Limitations

  • All parameters are provisional. Construction costs, land values (farmland prices are used as a lower bound) and the grid connection cost are simplified national assumptions.
  • Electricity prices are average industrial prices from utilities serving each county (2022–24). Large data centers often negotiate special rates that are not public.
  • Grid carbon uses regional average emission rates, not the extra (marginal) emissions a new load would cause.
  • Some inputs are estimated (Alaska and Hawaii climate; electricity prices set to the state average where local data are missing or unrepresentative). The county profile flags these.
  • Water at power plants is estimated from published averages by fuel and cooling type; reservoir evaporation from hydropower is excluded.
  • Data-center locations come from OpenStreetMap (PNNL IM3 atlas) and Epoch AI; both are incomplete.
  • The map colour scale is set by the lower 48 states and DC; Alaska and Hawaii values beyond it show the end colour (in the paper, they have their own scales).
  • These are indicators for comparison, not forecasts of what any project will cost, and not causal estimates of impacts.

Everyday equivalents

The county profile translates one year of the campus into familiar units:

  • US homes: annual electricity of the campus ÷ 10,359 kWh, the average annual electricity use of a US residential customer in 2024 (U.S. Energy Information Administration, Form EIA-861 2024: residential sales ÷ residential customers. The same calculation gives 10,791 kWh for 2022, the value in EIA's FAQ "How much electricity does an American home use?").
  • Passenger cars: annual CO₂ of the campus ÷ 4.6 t, the CO₂ of a typical passenger vehicle per year (US EPA, Greenhouse Gas Equivalencies Calculator).
  • Football fields: site acres ÷ 1.32 acres, an American football field including end zones (360 × 160 ft).
  • State residential electricity price, 2014 and 2024: all residential revenue ÷ residential sales by state (US EIA Form 861). Shown in nominal dollars and after inflation, using the US Bureau of Labor Statistics consumer price index (CPI-U, all items, US city average, annual averages: 236.736 in 2014 and 313.689 in 2024, a rise of 32.5%). Nationally the residential price rose 32% in nominal terms and fell about 1% after inflation. Shown as context only; it does not measure any campus's effect on bills.
  • Jobs: operations staff is a model assumption (100 per 100 MW, scaled with size). Construction-phase jobs (about 1,688 local jobs over 18–24 months, including indirect jobs) come from an industry-sponsored report: US Chamber of Commerce Technology Engagement Center (2017), Data Centers: Jobs and Opportunities in Communities Nationwide.
  • Water supply comparison: USGS county public-supply withdrawals, 2015 (latest county release).
  • Olympic pools: annual water ÷ 2,500 m³, the volume of a nominal Olympic pool (50 m × 25 m × 2 m). Shown for on-site cooling water and for on-site plus power-plant water.

Method in brief

For each county: construction cost per MW, adjusted for local construction wages, plus land, a hazard allowance and a grid connection; plus ten years of electricity (energy use rises in hot climates through the cooling overhead, PUE), carbon, water and staff, discounted at 5% per year. The token layer converts the facility cost into dollars per megawatt-hour of computing and then into the cost of 1M tokens using published estimates of energy per token (central: 550 Wh per 1M output tokens for closed frontier models, 150 Wh for open-weight models). Full methods, sources and sensitivity analyses are in the working paper.
